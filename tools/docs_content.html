Music Glasses Mark 1 — music and calls in your own tortoiseshell frames

Rounded-square tortoiseshell acetate frames that look completely normal from the front. Two slim gold hinge pods sit 17 mm back on the wire temples, each holding a MAX98357A I²S amp, a 15 × 11 mm micro-speaker on the inner face and three flush touch inlays. Two tortoise ear pods ride on the acetate ear tips, under the hair: the right one carries an Adafruit QT Py ESP32 Pico (classic Bluetooth A2DP + HFP + BLE), the USB-C charger, the power switch and an SPH0645 I²S mic at its front edge, aimed down the jaw line; the left one carries a 120 mAh cell. A Flutter app talks to the glasses over BLE and helps you find them.

1 · MusicBluetooth musicA2DP sink "Muse Glasses" + AVRCP: stereo from your phone, one amp + speaker per side.
2 · CallsPhone callsHands-Free Profile (HFP 1.7 client): CVSD 8 kHz or mSBC 16 kHz wideband, mic in the right ear pod.
3 · TouchTap · long-press · swipeTap = play/pause or answer/hang up. Long-press = reject. Swipe = volume (call volume in a call).
4 · AppCompanion appFlutter + flutter_blue_plus: now playing, call state, volume, EQ, battery, settings.
5 · FindFind my glassesHotter/colder RSSI meter, where your phone was at the last disconnect, and a Play-sound chime that ignores mute.
6 · DiscreetInvisible from the frontNothing on the frame front; the cross-frame wire hides behind the top rim; the electronics hide under the hair.

Soldering 101

Soldering means melting a metal alloy so it flows between two pieces of metal and joins them, electrically and mechanically. The rule that fixes 90 % of problems: heat the parts, not the solder. The solder should melt because it touches the hot pad and leg, not because it touches the iron.

  1. Set up. Safety glasses on, window open, fan pulling the smoke away. Iron in its stand, brass cleaner next to it. Set 330 °C for leaded solder (350–370 °C for lead-free).
  2. Tin the tip (fig. 1). Wipe it in the brass, then melt a little solder onto it. A shiny tip moves heat; a black, dull one doesn't. Re-tin before you put the iron back in the stand.
  3. Make a joint (figs. 2–4). Touch the tip to the pad and the leg for 1–2 s, feed solder into the far side of the joint until it forms a small cone, take the solder away, then the iron. 2–4 s in total. Don't blow on it or move it while it sets.
  4. Practice kit. Build the WeevilEye or Mini Airplane. Compare every joint with the good-vs-bad picture, fix the bad ones with flux + reheat or wick (fig. 8).
  5. Headers on the real boards. Push the header strip into the breadboard (long pins down), drop the QT Py on top, solder one corner pin, check it's straight, then do the rest. Same for the amp's and the mic's headers. The breadboard keeps the pins straight.
  6. Thin wire (figs. 5–6). Strip 2–3 mm of 30 AWG, twist, flux, tin. Tin the pad, lay the wire on, hold it with tweezers, touch 1 s. Tug gently: a good joint doesn't move.
  7. Magnet wire (fig. 7, week 4). Practise 10 times on scrap wire before the real harness. Burn the enamel off in a hot solder blob, or scrape it. Then beep it with the multimeter.
Safety. The tip is ~350 °C: always put the iron back in its stand, never on the desk, and never catch a falling iron. Run cool water over a burn for 10–20 min. Ventilate (the smoke is flux, not lead, but it irritates). Wash your hands after soldering and before eating, especially with leaded solder; don't eat or drink at the bench. LiPo batteries: never short the two leads (cut and insulate one wire at a time), never puncture, bend or squeeze the cell, never charge it unattended or above 4.2 V, use only a protected cell and the 100 mA charger, and don't charge while wearing. If a cell swells, gets hot or smells sweet, stop using it and put it on a non-flammable surface outdoors.

Software setup from zero

The firmware is the program that runs on the QT Py. You'll install a free editor, teach it about ESP32 boards, add two libraries, then press Upload. It takes about 30–45 minutes the first time, mostly downloads.

  1. Install Arduino IDE 2. Download it from arduino.cc/en/software (Windows, macOS or Linux) and open it.
  2. Add the ESP32 boards URL. File → Preferences (macOS: Arduino IDE → Settings) → Additional boards manager URLs, paste:
    https://espressif.github.io/arduino-esp32/package_esp32_index.json → OK. (Espressif's install guide)
  3. Install the esp32 core. Tools → Board → Boards Manager, search esp32, pick esp32 by Espressif Systems, choose version 3.3.x (tested: 3.3.12) and Install. It's a big download (~10 min).
  4. Select the board. Tools → Board → esp32 → Adafruit QT Py ESP32 (that's the Pico). Leave Partition Scheme on the default (3 MB app). (Adafruit's setup page)
  5. Install the two libraries (GitHub zips). Download ESP32-A2DP v1.8.11 .zip and arduino-audio-tools v1.2.6 .zip. In the IDE: Sketch → Include Library → Add .ZIP Library… and pick each zip in turn. Don't unzip them first.
  6. Open the sketch. Unzip this project, then File → Open → firmware/music_glasses/music_glasses.ino (the folder name must match the file name, as it does here).
  7. Plug in USB-C. Use a data cable. Tools → Port: pick the new entry (Windows COM3…, macOS /dev/cu.usbserial-…, cu.usbmodem… or cu.wchusbserial…, Linux /dev/ttyACM0 or ttyUSB0).
  8. Upload. Click the → arrow. It compiles (1–3 min the first time), then shows Writing at 0x… percentages and Hard resetting via RTS pin…. Done.
  9. Read the Serial Monitor. Tools → Serial Monitor, set 115200 baud, press the QT Py's RST button. You should see Muse Glasses ready, free heap ….

Desk-test checkpoints

Test at every stage before you move on. Tick them off: progress is saved in this browser.

Week 1 · bench & practice

  1. Iron heats to the set temperature in ~10–20 s on the PD charger (the Pinecil screen shows it). Solder melts instantly on the tinned tip.
  2. Practice kit works (the LEDs light). All joints look like the GOOD picture.
  3. Multimeter beeps when you touch the two probes together (continuity mode, the ))) symbol). Beep one header pin against its pad: beep. Against the next pin: silence (no bridge).

Week 2 · music on the breadboard

  1. Upload works and the Serial Monitor says Muse Glasses ready, free heap … (the number should be well above 30000).
  2. Wire it with the board unplugged: 5V→VIN, GND→GND, A0→BCLK, A1→LRC, RX→DIN; GAIN and SD unconnected; speaker into the + / − terminal block. Double-check 5V and GND before you plug in.
  3. Pair. Phone → Bluetooth → Muse Glasses. Serial: A2DP connected, free heap ….
  4. Hear music from the speaker, at a level that follows the phone's volume buttons. Play/pause on the phone works. Some hiss with no music is normal; crackle isn't (see troubleshooting).

Week 3 · touch, then mic + calls

  1. Tap any pad briefly (40–300 ms): music pauses / plays.
  2. Swipe a finger across R3→R2→R1 (rear to front, toward the lens): volume up a step. The other way: down.
  3. Call. Call your phone from another phone (or call a friend). Tap a pad to answer. Serial: HFP state … then SCO mSBC 16k (or CVSD 8k). You hear the caller from the speaker; they hear you when you talk near the mic.
  4. Hang up with a tap; while it's ringing, a ≥ 0.8 s press rejects the call. After the call, music resumes.
  5. Optional: the BLE app / nRF Connect sees the glasses' service (see GATT table).

Week 4 · in the frames

  1. Dry fit the printed pods on her frames and check the front view in a mirror before any soldering.
  2. Beep every harness wire end to end and against its neighbours before it goes behind the rim cover.
  3. Test on USB first (no battery): pairing, both speakers, touch and a call, exactly like weeks 2–3.
  4. Battery last. Check polarity with the meter (red + / black −), switch off, connect, insulate. Switch on: Serial shows the ready line; unplug USB and it keeps playing.

Troubleshooting checklist

No soundPairs, but silent
  • VIN on 5V (not 3V) and GND connected?
  • BCLK/LRC/DIN not swapped (A0→BCLK, A1→LRC, RX→DIN)?
  • SD not tied to GND (that shuts the amp down)?
  • Speaker screws tight on bare wire, not on insulation?
  • Phone volume up and "Muse Glasses" picked as the audio output?
BluetoothWon't show up / won't pair
  • Serial shows the ready line? If not: re-upload, press RST.
  • "Forget" old Muse Glasses on the phone and pair again.
  • Only one phone at a time: switch Bluetooth off on the other.
  • Keep the board within a few metres while testing.
NoiseCrackle, pops, dropouts
  • Push the jumpers fully in; wiggle each one while music plays.
  • Use a short USB cable straight into the computer or a wall charger, not a hub.
  • Free heap in Serial under ~20000? See Audio & Bluetooth.
TouchRandom pauses or no response
  • Press RST with your hands off the pads (it calibrates at start-up).
  • Pads not touching each other, jumpers in the right pins (A2, SDA, SCL)?
  • Laptop on battery vs charger changes readings: test both.
CallsThey can't hear you
  • Mic SEL to GND, 3V (not 5V) to the mic.
  • DOUT on the blue STEMMA QT wire (GPIO22).
  • BCLK/LRCL shared with the amp's rows.
  • Talk within ~10 cm; raise MIC_GAIN in the sketch if quiet.
BoardResets, hot parts, weird smells
  • Unplug at once. Look for a solder bridge or a 5V-to-GND jumper.
  • Beep 5V against GND with USB unplugged: it must not beep.
  • Battery hot or puffy: stop, see the LiPo safety box above.

Glossary, in plain words

GPIO
"General-purpose input/output": one of the QT Py's metal pins the program can switch on/off or read. GPIO27 = pin number 27 inside the chip (printed "A2" on the board).
Pad
A small copper spot on a board where you solder a wire or a pin.
Header pins
A strip of metal pins you solder onto a board so it can plug into a breadboard.
Breadboard
A plastic board full of holes connected in rows: push parts and jumper wires in, no solder.
Jumper wire
A short wire with a pin on each end, for breadboards.
Microcontroller · QT Py ESP32 Pico
A tiny computer on a chip that runs one program. Ours does the Bluetooth, reads the touch pads and sends sound to the amps.
Firmware · sketch
The program on the microcontroller. In Arduino a program is called a sketch (.ino file).
Library
Ready-made code someone else wrote (here: Bluetooth audio) that our sketch uses.
Core / board package
The add-on that teaches the Arduino IDE how to build programs for ESP32 chips.
Serial Monitor
A window in the Arduino IDE that shows text messages the board sends over USB. Your best debugging tool.
Bluetooth Classic vs BLE
Classic Bluetooth carries the audio (music and calls). BLE (Bluetooth Low Energy) is a low-power side channel the app uses for buttons, battery and Find my glasses.
A2DP
The Bluetooth "profile" for streaming music in stereo from your phone.
AVRCP
The profile for remote control: play, pause, volume, song titles.
HFP
Hands-Free Profile: phone calls (voice both ways, answer/hang up).
I²S
"Inter-IC Sound": a 3-wire digital audio connection (bit clock BCLK, left/right clock LRC, data DIN/DOUT) between the QT Py and the amps/mic.
Amp · MAX98357A
Turns the digital I²S audio into a signal strong enough to drive a speaker.
MEMS mic · SPH0645
A tiny digital microphone chip that outputs I²S.
LiPo
Lithium-polymer battery: light and small, but must never be shorted, punctured or overcharged (max 4.2 V).
Flux
A cleaning chemical (built into rosin-core solder, extra from the pen) that lets solder flow and stick.
Rosin-core solder
Solder wire with flux inside. 60/40 = 60 % tin, 40 % lead. Lead-free (SAC305) needs a hotter iron.
Tinning
Coating a tip, wire or pad with a thin layer of solder first, so the final joint is quick.
Cold joint
A dull, lumpy joint that didn't get hot enough. It may work today and fail tomorrow. Reheat it with flux.
Bridge
Solder accidentally joining two pads that shouldn't touch: a short circuit.
Solder wick
Copper braid that soaks up melted solder to undo mistakes.
AWG
Wire thickness; bigger number = thinner. 30 AWG ≈ 0.25 mm, 38 AWG magnet wire ≈ 0.1 mm (hair-thin).
Magnet wire
Solid copper wire insulated by a thin enamel coating instead of plastic. Used for the hidden harness because it's so thin.
Continuity
Multimeter mode that beeps when two points are electrically connected.
Short circuit
An accidental connection (usually + to −) that lets too much current flow. It can heat parts or a battery fast.
Kapton
Orange heat-proof tape (polyimide) that insulates and protects.
USB-C PD
Power Delivery: lets a USB-C charger give more than 5 V (the Pinecil wants 12–20 V).
Boot mode
A special start-up mode for receiving new firmware (hold BOOT, tap RST).

Inconspicuous by design

The wearer's hair covers the temples and ears, so the layout pushes everything that can move back there:

  • Frame front: nothing electronic, no wires, no LEDs. The cross-frame ribbon runs on the back face of the top rim, under a tortoise cover strip. Cleaner (but irreversible) option: route a 1 mm groove along the inside top edge of the rim and fill it with tinted UV resin.
  • Hinge pods: as slim (6.2 mm) and as far back (17 mm behind the hinge) as the amp breakout allows; outer face flush with the end piece; the head-side lid is tortoise. Speakers fire from the inner face; no grille on show. Gold paint, like the wire temples.
  • Behind-ear pods: QT Py, charger, switch, USB-C, battery and the mic. Tortoise, like the ear tips. Hidden by hair.
  • What still can't move back: the amps + speakers (they must sit near the ear canal, and moving the amps would add 4 more wires per side down the temple) and the touch pads (where a finger naturally lands).

The hinge pods are taller than the wire temple by design: the amp breakout (19 × 18 mm) sets their height, but from the front they stay inside the end piece's outline. Use a non-conductive finish over the touch pads.

  1. Measure your frames. Wire temple diameter (~1.5–1.8 mm), hinge-to-ear-tip distance, ear-tip thickness. Adjust the pod channels before printing.
  2. Print. Resin or PLA at 0.12 mm layers. Sand, prime, dry-fit on your head: pods must clear your temples, ear pods sit behind the ears. Look at it face-on in a mirror: nothing should show past the end pieces.
  3. Pin the hinges. Wires crossing a folding hinge fatigue fast. For v1 pin the hinges open (thread-lock the hinge screws) or leave a soft silicone-wire loop.

7 Touch inlays & gestures

Three copper-tape electrodes per hinge pod, inlaid flush in a 0.1 mm recess on the outer face and painted over. The ESP32 measures each pad's capacitance with touchRead().

Long-pressReject callOne pad held ≥ 0.8 s while ringing. Ignored otherwise.
Swipe → / ←Volume ±Media ±8 of 127; during a call HFP speaker volume ±1 of 15 (synced to the phone). Invert in the app.
  1. Cut & place. Three ~11 mm pads with 1.5 mm gaps; a chevron edge lets a finger overlap two pads mid-swipe.
  2. Solder the tabs on the inside of the pod wall, then seal the slot with UV resin.
  3. Paint. Non-conductive finish only (mica pigments are fine; aluminium-flake metallics are not — check with a meter).
  4. Tune. Firmware triggers at −15 % and releases at −10 % of a 64-sample baseline, with a 150 ms lockout. After painting, recalibrate from the app (Config bit 1).

8 Audio & Bluetooth stack

The original ESP32 (in the QT Py ESP32 Pico) runs classic Bluetooth (A2DP + AVRCP + HFP) and BLE on one chip. Music flows as A2DP (SBC) → 3-band EQ → I²S → 2 × MAX98357A. Each amp picks its channel from the stereo frame by its SD voltage.

  • Core: Arduino-ESP32 3.3.x · board "Adafruit QT Py ESP32" · default 3 MB app partition (8 MB flash).
  • Libraries: ESP32-A2DP 1.8.10+ and arduino-audio-tools. BLE on the core's Bluedroid stack — NimBLE is reported to crash alongside A2DP.
  • Mode: BTDM (dual mode). No Wi-Fi, so no OTA: flash over USB.
  • RAM is tight: keep one custom GATT service, watch the free heap printed on connect and on every call, switch to BluetoothA2DPSinkQueued only if you hear dropouts.

9 Phone calls (HFP) & the mic

Route: plain Arduino, no ESP-IDF rebuild

I checked the prebuilt libraries that ship with Arduino-ESP32 3.3.12 (ESP-IDF 5.5.5): its sdkconfig.h for the ESP32 already sets CONFIG_BT_HFP_ENABLE, CONFIG_BT_HFP_CLIENT_ENABLE, CONFIG_BT_HFP_AUDIO_DATA_PATH_HCI, CONFIG_BT_HFP_WBS_ENABLE, CONFIG_BTDM_CTRL_BR_EDR_SCO_DATA_PATH_HCI and leaves CONFIG_BT_HFP_USE_EXTERNAL_CODEC off, so Bluedroid's own CVSD/mSBC codec is in. The pschatzmann ESP32-A2DP library only covers A2DP + AVRCP, so the sketch calls the raw esp_hf_client_* API next to it on the same Bluedroid instance. No ESP-IDF project or custom sdkconfig is needed.

  • Init: after a2dp.start() → set Class of Device (Audio, hands-free, telephony) → esp_hf_client_register_callback → esp_hf_client_init → esp_hf_client_register_data_callback(in, out).
  • Audio path: SCO over HCI. With the internal codec the stack hands the app 16-bit mono PCM (8 kHz CVSD or 16 kHz mSBC) in the incoming callback and pulls mic PCM through the outgoing callback after esp_hf_client_outgoing_data_ready(). These are ESP-IDF's "legacy" data callbacks (esp_hf_client_legacy_api.h): still present and linked in 5.5, but Espressif says the internal codec will be removed in a later IDF. Then switch to esp_hf_client_register_audio_data_callback + your own CVSD/mSBC codec.
  • Call control: CIND call / call-setup events → state (idle, incoming, outgoing, active), notified over GATT. Tap → esp_hf_client_answer_call() or hang up (esp_hf_client_reject_call() = AT+CHUP), long-press → reject, swipe → esp_hf_client_volume_update(SPK, 0–15). Phone-side volume changes (+VGS) update the local level.
  • I²S switching: music = TX only, 44.1 kHz/16-bit. When SCO comes up the firmware reopens I²S0 full duplex at 32 kHz/32-bit (the SPH0645 needs a 2.048–4.096 MHz bit clock), upsamples the far end ×2 (mSBC) or ×4 (CVSD) for the speakers and averages the mic down to 16/8 kHz. It switches back when SCO drops. The phone pauses A2DP during a call.
  • Echo: open-ear speakers ~3 cm from the ear and a mic ~6 cm behind them will leak. The phone's own AEC/NR stays on (the glasses do not send AT+NREC=0); the firmware adds half-duplex ducking (mic −12 dB for 150 ms whenever the far end is louder than a threshold) and keeps the call volume moderate. No on-chip AEC: ESP-SR/AEC doesn't fit next to A2DP + BLE on this chip.
  • RAM: 46.9 KB static (14 %), plus 2 × 4 KB ring buffers and a 4 KB audio task at runtime; Bluedroid HFP itself takes ~15–20 KB of heap when connected (estimate). The free heap prints on HFP connect and every SCO start: keep it above ~30 KB.

The mic: behind the ear, for looks

Adafruit I2S MEMS Microphone Breakout, SPH0645LM4H (#3421, $6.95) glued to the lower-front wall of the right ear pod, its bottom port over a 1 mm hole with a short printed horn, aimed forward-down along the jaw line. Wiring (all local): BCLK → A0, LRCL → A1 (shared with the amps), DOUT → GPIO22 (SDA1 on the STEMMA QT connector, not a strapping pin), SEL → GND, 3V, GND.

Trade-off: ~9–11 cm from the mouth instead of ~3 cm on a boom means ~10 dB less voice level and more room sound. Mitigations in this build: a sensitive MEMS mic (−26 dBFS @ 94 dB SPL), 6× digital gain (MIC_GAIN), the directional horn, foam sealing so it hears outside not inside the pod, and the phone's noise reduction. If callers still say you're far away, the next step is a second mic for beamforming. That costs RAM and another wire, so it's left out of v1.

10 Battery & charging

Protected 120 mAh 401230 cell in the left ear pod → its + lead crosses the frame (behind the top rim) to the slide switch in the right ear pod → switch OUT → QT Py VBAT+ (diode-protected, 3–6 V) and both amps' VIN. The Micro-Lipo charger (100 mA default, right for 120 mAh) sits on the cell side of the switch and is fed from the QT Py's 5 V pad, so the one USB-C port flashes and charges.

  1. Check polarity on the cell leads before soldering; insulate each joint immediately.
  2. Battery sense. 100 k / 100 k divider from switched VBAT to A3 (GPIO15, ADC2, strapping pin). Firmware maps 3.3–4.2 V to % every 5 s.
  3. Run time (estimates). Music ~100–130 mA → ~1 h. Calls ~110–150 mA (SCO keeps the radio busier, plus the mic and 32 kHz I²S) → ~45–60 min of talk. Connected idle ~25–40 mA → ~3–4 h of standby. 350 mAh ≈ 3×.
  4. Safety. Don't charge while wearing; keep the cell from being crushed in the ear pod; never use an unprotected cell.

Three tools in the app's Find screen, none of which need a server or an account:

Limits: the glasses must be switched on and within BLE range (~10–30 m indoors). If the battery is flat or the switch is off there's nothing to hear and only "last seen" helps. On iOS, background BLE scanning is throttled and needs the bluetooth-central background mode; the disconnect location needs "While Using" (or "Always") location permission, so the app saves it when it next runs if the OS suspended it.

15 Build order (bench first)

  1. Breadboard audio. QT Py + one amp + speaker, pair a phone and play. Add the second amp with the 330 kΩ and confirm stereo.
  2. AVRCP. Check play/pause, volume sync and metadata on both iPhone and Android.
  3. Touch. Copper pads on a printed pod blank: log raw values, tune thresholds.
  4. Calls. Add the SPH0645 on the breadboard (GPIO22), place a call from the phone: check answer / hang up / reject, both directions of audio, mSBC vs CVSD, and the free heap. Then music → call → music transitions.
  5. BLE. Test the GATT with nRF Connect while streaming; watch free heap.
  6. App. Connect + Now Playing first, then call state, Find (RSSI, last seen, Play sound), EQ and Settings.
  7. Power. Cell, charger, switch; measure streaming current and run a full play-time test.
  8. Frame integration. The 14 animated steps of this guide.

16 Risks & caveats

  • Fit: the QT Py (22 × 18 mm) and amp breakouts (19 × 18 mm) don't fit thin wire temples, so v1 accepts clip-on hinge and ear-tip pods.
  • Battery life: ~1 h of music or ~45–60 min of calls on 120 mAh (estimates); ~3× with a 350 mAh cell in a longer left pod.
  • Call quality: the mic is ~10 cm from the mouth (behind the ear, for looks): lower voice level and more room noise than earbuds. Tune MIC_GAIN; keep the phone's noise reduction on.
  • Echo: open-ear speakers leak into the mic. Mitigated by the phone's AEC + firmware half-duplex ducking + moderate call volume; expect some double-talk clipping.
  • iOS: HFP calls work, and iOS sends calls to the glasses when they're the selected audio route (it normally picks the last connected HFP device). iOS won't show the Classic link to apps, so the app relies on the GATT call state. Background BLE scanning is throttled, and the last-seen location depends on the location permission. Android behaves more predictably.
  • Legacy HFP data API: uses ESP-IDF's internal codec + legacy PCM callbacks; a future core may drop them (then: external-codec callbacks + own CVSD/mSBC).
  • Hinge wiring fatigues: pin the hinges or leave silicone loops.
  • Open-ear sound: these speakers resonate near 950 Hz — thin bass, some leakage. Keep gain low.
  • Touch through paint: non-conductive finish only; isolate the pads from the metal wire temple (Kapton under the pod channel).
  • Stack stability: A2DP + BLE is RAM-tight; Wi-Fi is off the table.
  • LiPo near the head: protected cell, 100 mA charge, no charging while worn.
  • 9 vs 8 cross-frame wires: see the harness section.
